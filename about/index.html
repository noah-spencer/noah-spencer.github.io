My work is motivated by two ideas:

  • First, that lineages of organisms with extreme and exceptional characteristics are valuable study systems not just as individually remarkable evolutionary stories but also as a means to understand fundamental constraints on evolutionary change and diversification.
  • Second, that much of the most conceptually important biological diversity and evolutionary change occurs at the level of the cell.

One biological phenomenon at the confluence of these two ideas is endosymbiosis: broadly, a cell or cells (endosymbionts) living stably inside of a different cell (the host cell). This process gave rise to mitochondria and plastids (each descended from an ancient bacterial ancestor) and in turn reshaped the biosphere at least two times over. However, because very little trace remains of plastids and (especially) mitochondria from before they were cemented as bona fide cell organelles, much of our understanding of endosymbiosis as an evolutionary and cell biological process necessarily comes from more recent endosymbioses. These symbioses vary widely in age, the ancestry of the organisms involved, and the effects (beneficial or otherwise) on the host cell.

Most of my research so far concerns bacterial endosymbionts of insects, beginning in 2017 with my work on tsetse flies under Dr. Rita Rio in my home state of West Virginia. Shortly after receiving my B.S. from West Virginia University in 2021, I joined my current advisor as an Evolutionary Biology PhD student at Arizona State University, where I currently study endosymbionts of cicadas.
